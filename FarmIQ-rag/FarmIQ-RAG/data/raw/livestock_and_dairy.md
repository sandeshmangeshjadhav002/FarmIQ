# Livestock and Dairy Management

FarmIQ Starter Reference — feeding, health, and clean milk production for
cattle and buffalo. FarmIQ does not name veterinary medicines or give doses;
for any treatment decision, the animal's condition must be examined by a
veterinarian.

## Feeding — the biggest lever on milk yield

A milch animal needs four things, and shortfall in any one of them limits
production. Typical broad figures for an adult crossbred cow or buffalo, to be
adjusted for body weight, breed, and the milk she is giving — confirm your
ration with the local animal husbandry officer or a qualified nutritionist:

- **Green fodder:** roughly 25–30 kg per day. This is the cheapest source of
  energy and the base of the ration.
- **Dry fodder** (straw, hay): roughly 5–6 kg per day, for rumen health and
  fibre. Do not drop dry fodder entirely, however much green fodder you have.
- **Concentrate** (compound feed, oil cakes, cereal by-products): roughly 1 kg
  for every 2.5–3 litres of milk above maintenance, introduced or withdrawn
  gradually rather than in sudden jumps.
- **Mineral mixture and common salt:** a small daily allowance (roughly
  50–100 g of mineral mixture). Mineral support is often the cheapest way to
  fix unexplained fertility and yield problems.
- **Water: 60–100 litres per day**, freely available. A high-yielding animal
  needs water roughly three to four times the volume of milk she produces, and
  water shortage reduces milk immediately and sharply.

Feeding principles:

- **Balanced ration beats extra quantity.** Excess concentrate without
  adequate fibre causes digestive upset and costs more than it earns.
- **Green fodder should be mixed** — a pure or excess lush legume (lucerne,
  berseem) can cause bloat. Mix cereals such as maize or sorghum with legumes.
- **Never feed mouldy, musty, or spoiled fodder** and never feed fodder that
  has been sprayed recently; it can kill the animal.
- **Change the ration gradually** over a week or more. Sudden changes cause
  off-feed and a milk drop.
- During the **dry period** the animal must be fed well for the next lactation,
  and in the transition period around calving the concentrate is increased
  gradually. Good dry-period feeding pays back more than anything else.
- Feed at fixed times so the animal settles into a routine; feed during cooler
  hours in summer.

## Factors that decide milk yield

1. **Genetics** — the animal's breed and pedigree set the ceiling.
2. **Feed and water** — the most common and most fixable limit.
3. **Lactation stage** — yield rises in early lactation and declines later; it
   is normal for yield to fall as the lactation advances.
4. **Calving interval and dry period** — an animal that calves too late loses
   the whole year's income. Aim for a steady cycle and about a 60-day dry
   period.
5. **Heat stress** — above roughly 30 °C, intake and yield fall. Provide shade,
   ventilation, sprinklers, and extra water, and shift feeding to cooler hours.
6. **Health** — mastitis, worms, foot problems, and mineral deficiency all cut
   yield. Regular monitoring is cheaper than treatment.
7. **Milking routine** — regular timings, gentle full-hand milking, and a calm
   animal.

## Housing and comfort

- Give each animal enough floor space, and keep the floor dry, non-slippery,
  and clean. Wet, filthy floors are the main cause of hoof problems and
  mastitis.
- Ensure ventilation and shade; a hot, airless shed cuts yield and reduces
  fertility.
- Provide clean drinking water close to the shed, and clean the water trough
  daily.
- Keep the cattle shed separate from the fodder store, and manage dung and
  urine through a proper pit or compost — a dirty shed is a disease reservoir.
- Keep records on the wall or in a book: calving date, milk yield, feed,
  vaccination, and treatment. Records are how problems get found early.

## Health: what to watch for

**Emergency — call a veterinarian immediately:**

- **Bloat:** the left flank is hugely distended, the animal is uncomfortable,
  stamps, strains, or stops ruminating. This is life-threatening within hours.
  Do not delay and do not try home remedies on a bloated animal.
- **Down and unable to rise**, especially around calving.
- **Difficult or prolonged calving.**
- **High fever with drooling, blisters in the mouth or on the feet** —
  possible foot-and-mouth disease; isolate the animal at once and inform the
  veterinary officer, because it spreads to the whole herd and to neighbouring
  villages.

**Watch and act early:**

- **Mastitis:** a quarter that is hot, hard, or painful, or milk with clots,
  flakes, or a watery or blood-tinged appearance. Test with a strip cup at
  every milking. Segregate the milk from that quarter — never mix it into the
  bulk milk — and get the animal treated as advised.
- **Dullness, off-feed, reduced rumination:** the earliest sign of most
  problems, including acidosis from excess concentrate.
- **Sudden milk drop with reduced appetite and a sweet or acetone smell on the
  breath** — usually an early-lactation energy problem; get the animal examined.
- **Lameness and swollen feet.**
- **Unexplained weight loss, pale coat, or diarrhoea** — often worms; deworming
  is needed on a schedule, but the product and dose must come from the
  veterinarian.

**Prevention, always:**

- **Vaccination** against the diseases prevalent in your area — commonly
  foot-and-mouth disease, haemorrhagic septicaemia, and black quarter, with
  brucellosis vaccination for calves as advised. Follow the schedule given by
  your animal husbandry department and keep the certificate.
- **Deworming** on a schedule appropriate to your area — normally several times
  a year, more often in young stock.
- **Quarantine** a newly purchased animal away from the herd for a few weeks,
  and do not share water troughs in that period.
- **Monitor** the herd daily; a problem found early is far cheaper to treat.
- **Tick and fly control** in the shed and on the animals, since ticks carry
  serious diseases.
- **Get the animal insured** where schemes are available, and keep it
  identified and tagged as required.

## Clean milk production

Milk that is clean and cool earns more and keeps longer, and this is entirely
within your control:

1. Wash your hands and arms before milking, and keep the animal's udder and
   flanks clean and clipped.
2. Discard the first few streams of milk from each quarter into a separate
   strip cup — this is where the bacteria are highest, and it also shows
   mastitis.
3. Use a clean, dry cloth for the udder, and wash and dry it between animals
   rather than using one wet cloth on all.
4. Milking **preparation before** and **a teat dip after** milking reduce new
   mastitis infections more than any treatment. Use the dip recommended by your
   veterinarian.
5. Use stainless steel or aluminium buckets and cans — avoid plastic and
   galvanised containers for milk. Wash them with clean water and a proper
   dairy detergent, and dry them upside down.
6. Filter the milk and **cool it quickly** to about 4 °C. Cooling is what stops
   bacteria multiplying on the way to the collection centre.
7. **Never add water** to milk. It lowers quality, is detected at the dairy, and
   ruins your reputation as a supplier.
8. Milk in a clean, quiet place away from dung and dust, and keep your own
   head and hands away from the bucket.
