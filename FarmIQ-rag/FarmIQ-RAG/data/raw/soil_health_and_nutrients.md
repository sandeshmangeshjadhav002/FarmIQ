# Soil Health and Nutrient Management

FarmIQ Starter Reference — general agronomic guidance compiled from widely
accepted soil-science principles. Confirm field-specific decisions with a soil
test and your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or agricultural extension
officer.

## Why soil health matters

Healthy soil supplies water, air, and nutrients to crop roots, hosts beneficial
micro-organisms, and resists erosion. Managing soil is a long-term investment:
improvements build up over several seasons.

## Soil pH

Soil pH controls how easily plants take up nutrients. Most field crops grow
best in a slightly acidic to neutral range.

| pH range | Interpretation | Typical effect |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| Below 5.5 | Strongly acidic | Phosphorus locks up; aluminium toxicity risk |
| 5.5 – 6.0 | Moderately acidic | Good for many crops; some nutrients less available |
| 6.0 – 7.0 | Slightly acidic to neutral | Best general range for most crops |
| 7.0 – 8.0 | Neutral to slightly alkaline | Fine for many crops; iron/zinc less available |
| Above 8.0 | Alkaline | Iron, zinc, manganese deficiencies common |

- **Acidic soils** are commonly corrected with agricultural lime, applied well
  before sowing, based on a soil-test recommendation.
- **Alkaline soils** are usually managed with organic matter, gypsum where
  sodicity is the cause, and by choosing tolerant crops.
- Never guess the lime or gypsum dose: apply it according to a soil test.

## Soil organic matter

Organic matter improves water-holding capacity, structure, and nutrient
supply. Practical ways to raise it:

- Return crop residues to the field instead of burning them.
- Apply well-decomposed farmyard manure or compost.
- Grow green-manure crops (for example sunhemp or dhaincha) and incorporate
  them before flowering.
- Rotate cereals with legumes to fix nitrogen and break pest cycles.

## The primary nutrients: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium (NPK)

- **Nitrogen (N)** drives leaf and stem growth and gives leaves their green
  colour. Deficiency shows as pale, yellowing older leaves and stunted growth.
  Excess nitrogen causes excessive leafy growth, delayed maturity, and
  lodging.
- **Phosphorus (P)** supports root development, flowering, and seed formation.
  Deficiency shows as dull, dark green or purplish leaves and poor root growth.
- **Potassium (K)** improves water regulation, disease resistance, and grain or
  fruit quality. Deficiency commonly shows as scorching or browning at leaf
  margins on older leaves.

Apply NPK based on a soil test and the crop's recommended dose. Broadcasting
without a test risks both under-fertilising and wasting money on excess.

## Secondary and micronutrients

- **Sulphur** matters for oilseeds and pulses.
- **Zinc** deficiency is widespread in many Indian soils, especially in
  alkaline or continuously flooded (rice) conditions; it appears as
  interveinal whitening on young leaves.
- **Iron** deficiency (interveinal yellowing of young leaves) is common in
  calcareous, alkaline soils.
- **Boron** matters for flowering and fruit set.

Micronutrient problems are best confirmed by a soil or leaf test rather than
guessed from symptoms alone.

## Soil testing and the Soil Health Card

A soil test measures pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, and available
NPK plus micronutrients where required. Test before each major season where
possible, and collect samples from several spots in the field mixed together,
from the correct depth (usually the top 15 cm). Government Soil Health Cards
provide crop-wise fertiliser recommendations that should guide application.

## Practical soil-management checklist

1. Test the soil and follow the recommended fertiliser dose.
2. Keep the soil covered (residues or a cover crop) to reduce erosion.
3. Avoid working the soil when it is too wet, which damages structure.
4. Add organic matter every season.
5. Rotate crops, including legumes, to protect soil health and break pest
   cycles.
6. Correct pH and micronutrient problems before the main crop, not during it.
