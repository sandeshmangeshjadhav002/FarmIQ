# Vegetable and Horticulture Practices

FarmIQ Starter Reference — practical practices for common vegetables, and the
principles that apply to horticulture crops in general.

Spacing, variety, and dose figures vary by state and season. The figures below
are common recommendations for planning; always follow your state's Package of
Practices (PoP) for the exact rate.

## Principles that apply to every vegetable crop

- **Rotate crop families.** Do not grow the same family in the same plot in
  consecutive seasons — diseases and nematodes build up in the soil.
  - Solanaceae: tomato, brinjal, chilli, potato
  - Allium: onion, garlic
  - Cucurbits: cucumber, bitter gourd, bottle gourd, pumpkin
  - Crucifers: cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli
  - Legumes: pea, bean, cowpea
- **Raised beds and drainage.** Most vegetables are far more sensitive to
  waterlogging than field crops. Raised beds with proper drainage prevent root
  rot and wilting.
- **Mulch.** Plastic mulch controls weeds, saves water, and reduces fruit
  contact with the soil (less rot). Organic mulch — straw, crop residue — cools
  the soil and adds organic matter.
- **Drip and fertigation.** With drip irrigation you can supply small, frequent
  amounts of water and nutrients where the roots are. This saves water and
  gives better quality, especially for tomato, chilli, and onion.
- **Avoid excess nitrogen.** Lush, dark, soft growth attracts sucking pests,
  delays maturity, and reduces fruit set. A balanced dose is more profitable
  than a heavy one.
- **Harden seedlings before transplanting** by reducing water and shade for a
  few days, and transplant in the late afternoon or on a cloudy day so the
  plants establish without wilting.
- **Sanitation.** Remove and destroy infected plants, fallen fruit, and crop
  residue; many vegetable diseases survive in the debris between seasons.
- **Scout weekly.** Vegetable pests multiply quickly. Early detection is the
  difference between one spot spray and a lost crop.

## Tomato

- **Nursery:** raise seedlings in a raised, treated nursery bed; transplant
  about 25–30 days old, at the 4–6 leaf stage.
- **Spacing:** commonly about 60 × 45 cm for determinate varieties; give more
  room for indeterminate types.
- **Support:** stake or trellis the plants so fruit stays off the ground — this
  alone reduces fruit rot noticeably.
- **Irrigation:** keep moisture even, especially from flowering to fruit
  development. Uneven watering causes cracking and blossom end rot.
- **Watch for:** early blight and late blight, leaf curl virus (spread by
  whitefly), fruit borer, and blossom end rot.
- **Blossom end rot** is a dark, sunken base on the fruit caused by a calcium
  and water problem, not a disease. Fungicide will not fix it — even watering
  and correct nutrition will.
- **Harvest:** firm red fruit for local sale; mature green or turning for
  distant markets. Harvest in the morning and keep the fruit shaded.

## Onion

- **Season:** grown as kharif, late kharif, and rabi. Rabi onion generally
  stores best and gives the highest yield.
- **Nursery and transplanting:** transplant about 45–60 days old seedlings;
  commonly about 15 × 10 cm spacing.
- **Bolting:** plants going to seed reduce yield and store badly. Avoid late
  transplanting, and avoid cold spells and temperature shock in the nursery
  stage, which trigger bolting.
- **Watch for:** thrips (silvery streaks, curled and twisted leaves), purple
  blotch, and stemphylium blight in humid weather.
- **Stop irrigation about 15–20 days before harvest** so the bulbs cure
  properly, and harvest when the tops start to fall over.
- **Cure in shade for about 10–15 days** before storage, and store in a
  ventilated store. Good curing is the difference between three months and
  three weeks of storage life — see the storage reference.
- **Thick-necked and sprouted bulbs** should be sold first, not stored.

## Chilli

- **Spacing:** commonly about 60 × 45 cm.
- **Watch for:** thrips and mites (both cause upward curling and crinkling of
  young leaves), leaf curl virus, anthracnose and fruit rot in wet weather, and
  damping off in the nursery.
- **Fruit rot** usually starts where thrips or borer damage has created an
  entry point, so controlling the pest reduces the rot.
- Harvest green chilli for the fresh market, or allow full red colour for dry
  chilli; dry the red chilli thoroughly and dry it in shade first to preserve
  colour.

## Brinjal

- **Spacing:** commonly about 60 × 60 cm.
- **Shoot and fruit borer** is the main problem: the growing shoot wilts and
  droops, and fruit has entry holes and rots inside. Remove and destroy
  affected shoots and fruits at once, and do not leave them in the field.
- **Watch for:** little leaf (a virus-like problem with very small narrow
  leaves), and wilt.

## Okra

- **Spacing:** commonly about 45 × 30 cm.
- **Watch for:** yellow vein mosaic virus, spread by whitefly — the leaves show
  a yellow network of veins and plants are stunted. Remove infected plants and
  manage whitefly; there is no cure for an infected plant.
- **Fruit borer** damages the pods. Harvest young tender pods every 2–3 days;
  leaving over-mature pods on the plant reduces further yield.

## Banana

- **Planting material:** use disease-free tissue-culture plants or certified
  healthy suckers from a clean plot.
- **Watch for:** panama wilt (plants yellow, split at the base, and die — it
  survives in the soil for years, so avoid infected plots), bunchy top (spread
  by aphids, gives narrow bunched leaves and no proper bunch), and sigatoka
  leaf spot in humid weather.
- **Bunch care:** remove the male bud after the last hand has formed where
  recommended, and prop or stake plants carrying a heavy bunch.
- **Harvest** at the correct maturity for the market — three-quarter to full
  stage depending on transport distance.

## Other horticulture crops — quick notes

- **Grape:** downy mildew and powdery mildew are the key diseases; manage
  canopy ventilation and follow the spray calendar of your region strictly.
- **Pomegranate:** bacterial blight shows as oily, water-soaked spots on fruit
  and is the main limit on export and storage.
- **Citrus:** greening (patchy yellowing of the canopy and lopsided, bitter
  fruit), canker, and gummosis. Use certified planting material and control the
  insect vector.
- **Mango:** hopper and anthracnose at flowering in humid weather; soil and
  water management matters more than spraying after harvest.
- **Protected cultivation (polyhouse/shade net):** a great tool for vegetables
  and flowers, but humidity inside is high, so ventilation and disease
  monitoring are critical — fungal disease spreads fast in a closed structure.

## If you are starting a vegetable plot

1. Get a **soil test** first and add organic matter — vegetables repay good soil.
2. Choose **varieties suited to your season** and to the market you will sell to.
3. Plan a **rotation**, not a repeat of last season's crop family.
4. Install **drip with mulch** for the high-value crops where it pays back.
5. Keep a **weekly scouting routine** and act while the problem is small.
