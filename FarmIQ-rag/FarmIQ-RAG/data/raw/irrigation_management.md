# Irrigation and Water Management

FarmIQ Starter Reference — general agronomic guidance on irrigation. Confirm
schedules with your local extension service, and where available use
soil-moisture sensors or the FarmIQ irrigation module for field-specific advice.

## Principles of good irrigation

- Irrigate based on **soil moisture and crop need**, not on habit or a fixed
  calendar. Over-irrigation wastes water and leaches nutrients; under-
  irrigation stresses the crop and reduces yield.
- Water efficiently: apply water so it reaches the root zone and soaks in
  rather than running off.
- Match the method to the crop, soil, and water availability.

## Critical growth stages

Most crops are most sensitive to water stress at specific stages. Ensure
adequate moisture especially at:

- Germination and early establishment.
- Flowering.
- Grain, pod, or fruit filling.

Stress at these stages typically causes greater yield loss than stress at other
times.

## Irrigation methods

| Method | Best suited to | Notes |
|--------|----------------|-------|
| Flood / furrow | Close-growing field crops, level land | Simple but least efficient; large losses to runoff and evaporation |
| Sprinkler | Cereals, pulses, some vegetables on light soils | Good for sandy soils and undulating land; can favour leaf diseases |
| Drip | Row crops, vegetables, orchards, wide-spaced crops | Most water-efficient; delivers water at the root; supports fertigation |
| Sub-surface drip | High-value row crops, orchards | Very efficient; higher installation cost |

Drip and sprinkler systems can substantially reduce water use compared with
flood irrigation and are supported by several government schemes.

## Judging when to irrigate

- **Soil feel/hand test:** squeeze a handful of soil from the root zone. If it
  crumbles readily and does not hold together, moisture is likely low.
- **Soil-moisture sensors:** capacitive sensors report volumetric soil
  moisture. As a rough guide, many crops begin to experience stress when root-
  zone soil moisture falls below roughly one-third of field capacity, but the
  correct threshold depends on crop and soil.
- **Crop appearance:** wilting in the early morning indicates a real moisture
  deficit (as opposed to midday wilting, which can be normal heat response).
- **Evaporation:** hot, dry, windy weather increases water use; cool, humid,
  calm weather reduces it.

## Salinity and water quality

- Irrigating with saline water can build up salts in the root zone. Monitor
  electrical conductivity (EC) and avoid irrigating with water higher in salt
  than the crop can tolerate.
- On poorly drained soils, waterlogging damages roots. Improve drainage and
  avoid over-watering; extend the interval between irrigations where drainage
  is slow.

## Practical water-saving practices

1. Level the field so water spreads evenly.
2. Avoid irrigating in the hottest part of the day to reduce evaporation.
3. Mulch to retain soil moisture and suppress weeds.
4. Maintain channels and pipes to prevent leaks and seepage.
5. Consider drip or sprinkler where the crop and budget allow.
6. Keep records of irrigation timing and amounts to refine the schedule.

## Rainfall and irrigation

Use rainfall as part of the water budget: reduce irrigation after good rain and
check the effective root-zone moisture before the next planned irrigation.
Forecasts of heavy rain should be a cue to delay irrigation and avoid
waterlogging.
