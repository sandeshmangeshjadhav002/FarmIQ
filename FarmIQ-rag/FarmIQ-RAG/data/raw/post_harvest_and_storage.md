# Post-Harvest Handling and Storage

FarmIQ Starter Reference — reducing losses after harvest and storing produce
safely until you get a better price.

Post-harvest losses of 5–15% are common, and most of the loss happens through
moisture, insects, rodents, and bruising — all of which are preventable. Every
quintal saved is profit you do not have to grow.

## Harvest at the right stage

- Harvesting too early gives shrivelled grain, poor germination, and a poor
  price. Harvesting too late leads to shattering, lodging, sprouting in the
  ear, and field exposure to rain.
- Grain is normally harvested at high moisture and must be dried before
  storage. Do not bag and stack freshly harvested, damp grain.
- For fruits, maturity stage should be chosen by market distance: a crop going
  far needs to be harvested firmer and less ripe than one sold locally the
  same day.
- Harvest in the cooler part of the day where possible, and keep the produce
  out of direct sun after harvest.

## Dry properly — this is the main step

- Dry on a clean tarpaulin, mat, or concrete floor. **Never dry grain on the
  road or on bare soil** — it picks up grit, and road drying contaminates the
  produce and damages it under vehicle traffic.
- Spread in a thin layer and turn it regularly so drying is even. A thicker
  layer dries on top and stays wet underneath, which is where mould starts.
- Wetted or damp patches from a rain shower must be re-dried fully; do not
  mix partially dried grain with dry grain.
- Target safe moisture for storage is roughly 12–14% for cereals, 10–12% for
  pulses, and 8–10% for oilseeds. Confirm the exact figure for your crop with
  your local extension officer, and check with a moisture meter where available.
  This is the single most important number in storage.
- Sun drying also helps reduce insects, but it must not be so hot or prolonged
  that the grain cracks or the germination drops. Grain kept for seed must not
  be dried at high temperature.

## Clean and grade before storing

- Winnow, sieve, and remove stones, chaff, dust, broken grain, immature grain,
  and damaged grain. Broken and immature grain attracts insects and absorbs
  moisture.
- Grade into lots. Graded produce stores better, sells at a better rate, and
  makes it easy to sell part of the stock without opening everything.
- Do not store new grain on top of last season's grain, and never mix old
  stock with new.

## Store correctly

- Store in a dry, well-ventilated room or bin that is **rodent-proof,
  bird-proof, and water-tight**. Check the roof for leaks and the walls for
  cracks, and repair them before filling.
- Keep bags and grain **off the floor** on wooden dunnage or pallets, and
  **away from the walls** — never directly against a wall, and never under a
  leak.
- Leave an inspection alley between stacks so you can walk through and check
  every bag, and so air can move.
- Do not store seed, fertiliser, pesticide, oil, or kerosene in the same room
  as food grain. Fertiliser and pesticide fumes contaminate grain and reduce
  germination.
- Keep the store clean: sweep up spillage, remove old grain residue and empty
  bags, and repair cracks where insects hide. Stock a clean store rather than
  treating a dirty one.

## Insects and rodents in storage

Common storage pests include weevils in cereals, bruchid beetles in pulses,
grain moths, flour beetles, and rodents and birds.

- **Prevent:** clean, dry, airtight storage and clean bags are far more
  effective than any treatment.
- **Monitor:** inspect the store at least monthly, look inside a few bags from
  the middle of the stack, and watch for dust, webbing, and live insects.
- **Hermetic (airtight) storage:** airtight bags, drums, or metal bins for
  household and small-farmer quantities. The insects in the sealed grain use up
  the oxygen and die, so no chemical treatment is needed. Handle them carefully
  so they do not get punctured, and keep them sealed at all times.
- **Fumigation:** effective but genuinely dangerous. Fumigation must be done by
  a trained, licensed operator in a properly sealed store or chamber, following
  all safety rules, and never in a room attached to living quarters. FarmIQ
  does not give fumigation products, doses, or procedures — arrange this
  through a licensed pest control operator or your warehousing agency.
- **Rodents:** combine proofing (seal openings, metal sheets on doors),
  sanitation (no spilled grain, no rubbish heaps near the store), and trapping.
  Use rodenticides only with great care and strictly away from children, pets,
  and food; a rodenticide placed carelessly kills far more than rodents.
- For warehouse-scale storage, storing in a **registered (WDRA) warehouse**
  gives you professional storage plus a warehouse receipt you can pledge for a
  loan instead of selling early.

## Crop-specific notes

### Onion

- **Cure before storage:** dry the bulbs in shade (not in harsh direct sun) for
  about 10–15 days until the outer skin and the neck are properly dried. A
  wet neck is the main entry point for storage rot.
- Cut the neck, leaving a short stub; do not cut into the bulb. Do not bruise
  or drop the bulbs.
- Store in a **well-ventilated** structure with airflow at the bottom and the
  top — bottom-and-top ventilated stores work better than sealed rooms.
- Sort before storage and remove bulbs that are thick-necked, bolted,
  sprouted, damaged, or diseased. One rotting bulb spoils the bulbs around it.
- Good onion storage typically holds for about 3–5 months depending on variety
  and store. Check and re-sort periodically.
- Watch for neck rot and black mould in humid weather, and for sprouting in
  warm conditions.

### Potato

- Cure the tubers for about 10–15 days in a dark, ventilated, cool place so
  the skin sets and small wounds heal before long storage.
- Store in the dark. Light causes greening and a bitter taste, and greened
  potatoes should not be eaten.
- Cold storage extends shelf life greatly. Keep to the temperature advised for
  your intended use (table potato, seed potato, or processing), and ensure
  ventilation and proper stacking so air can move.
- Remove rotten tubers before storage; a single rotting tuber spreads
  infection through the whole lot.

### Tomato and vegetables

- Harvest at the stage the market wants — mature green or turning for distant
  markets, fully ripe for local sale the same day.
- **Use plastic crates instead of gunny bags.** Bags crush the produce at the
  bottom and the bruising turns into rot within days.
- Grade and remove damaged, cut, or diseased fruit before packing.
- Keep the produce shaded and move it quickly; for perishables, cooling after
  harvest extends marketable life more than anything else.
- For ripening, use only the approved, licensed ripening chambers.
  **Calcium carbide and other unapproved ripening agents are illegal and
  unsafe** — never use them, whatever a trader tells you. They leave harmful
  residue on the fruit you and your family eat.
- Do not store fruits that produce ripening gas together with vegetables that
  are sensitive to it; keep leafy vegetables and flowers away from ripe fruit.

## Before you store, ask three questions

1. Is the produce **dry enough** for safe storage? If not, dry it further.
2. Is the store **clean, dry, sealed against rodents**, and off the floor?
3. Is this crop **safe to hold** — and can you wait the number of weeks it
   needs without a cash emergency? If not, sell now rather than sell in a
   hurry later.
