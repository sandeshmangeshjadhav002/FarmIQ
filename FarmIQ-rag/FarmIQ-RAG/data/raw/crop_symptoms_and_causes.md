# Crop Symptoms, Likely Causes, and What to Check

FarmIQ Starter Reference — a symptom-first guide to help you work out what is
wrong with a crop before you spend money on a treatment.

Use it like this: find the symptom you can actually see, read the **possible
causes**, then do the **checks** listed. A description alone cannot confirm a
diagnosis — confirm in the field, and with your KVK if you are unsure.

## Disease or disorder?

- **Disease** (fungus, bacterium, virus, nematode) usually **spreads** — from
  plant to plant, from the lower leaves upwards, or along the wind or water
  direction, and the pattern gets worse over days.
- **Disorder** (nutrient shortage, water, heat, salt, spray damage) usually
  appears **evenly** across the field, on plants of the same age and variety,
  and often has no spreading edge.
- If the problem is a disorder, pesticides will not help. Correct the nutrient
  or the water first. This single check saves a lot of wasted sprays.

## Symptom guide

### Leaves turning yellow

- **Evenly pale or yellow on the older, lower leaves first** — nitrogen
  shortage (nitrogen moves to the new growth), or waterlogging.
  *Check:* is the field wet and compacted, or has it had heavy rain? Dig one
  plant and smell and look at the roots.
- **Yellow between the veins, on the young upper leaves** — iron, manganese,
  or zinc shortage, common in alkaline or over-limed soil.
  *Check:* soil pH from your Soil Health Card, and whether it appeared after
  heavy irrigation with alkaline water.
- **Mottled yellow-and-green mosaic or yellow patches in a pattern** — a
  **virus** (for example yellow mosaic in soybean, mungbean, and pulses; leaf
  curl in tomato and chilli).
  *Check:* look for whitefly or aphids on the underside of leaves, since these
  transmit the virus. Remove and destroy infected plants early.
- **Yellowing along the leaf edges, then browning** — potash shortage, salt
  build-up, or drought stress.
  *Check:* fertiliser history, irrigation water quality, and soil moisture.

### Wilting

- **Wilts in the afternoon heat but recovers at night** — water shortage,
  damaged roots, or soil compaction.
  *Check:* soil moisture at root depth, and whether a hardpan or plough pan
  limits root growth.
- **Wilts and does not recover; plants die one after another in a patch** —
  **wilt disease** (for example Fusarium or bacterial wilt) or root rot.
  *Check:* cut the stem lengthwise just above the soil line; look for brown
  discolouration of the internal tissue, and inspect the roots for rot.
  Remove affected plants, improve drainage, and rotate away from the same crop.

### Spots and blights on leaves

- **Small spots with a distinct border, often starting on older leaves and
  moving up** — fungal leaf spot or blight.
- **Round spots with concentric rings, mostly on older leaves** — early blight
  type disease.
  *Check:* start from the oldest leaves near the soil, and see whether the
  spots are enlarging with dark rings inside.
- **Water-soaked, greasy-looking patches that spread very fast, with a pale
  mould on the underside in damp weather** — late blight type disease, which
  can destroy a crop within days.
  *Check:* this spreads fastest in cool, humid, rainy weather and in dense
  crops; act immediately and remove badly infected plants.
- **White powdery coating on the leaf surface** — powdery mildew.
- **Yellowish patches on the upper surface with grey or violet mould on the
  underside** — downy mildew.
- **Orange, brown, or blackish raised pustules on leaves and stems that rub off
  as powder** — rust, a wind-borne disease that spreads rapidly.
- **Leaf margins and tips dying and turning brown while the rest stays green**
  — usually not a disease; check potash, salt, and drought.

### Holes and damage to leaves

- **Holes eaten in leaves, or leaves reduced to a net of veins** —
  caterpillars or beetle grubs. *Check:* the underside of leaves and the soil
  surface near the plant.
- **Silvery or white winding lines inside the leaf** — leaf miner.
- **Central shoot drying and dying ("dead heart"), which pulls out easily** —
  stem borer. *Check:* split the affected stem to find the larva.
- **Leaves stunted, curled, or deformed with sticky honeydew and ants** —
  sucking pests (aphids, whitefly, jassids, thrips, mites).
  *Check:* turn the leaf over and look closely; mites need a hand lens.
  Honeydew also encourages sooty mould, a black coating on the leaves.

### Problems on fruit, grain, and produce

- **Dry, white, or brown patches on the side facing the sun** — sunscald.
  *Check:* whether the crop lost its leaf cover, often after a pest attack or
  excessive pruning.
- **Brown or black sunken patches on fruit, often spreading from the base** —
  fruit rot, often entering where insects have damaged the skin.
- **Fruit cracking** — usually uneven irrigation, or heavy water after a dry
  spell. *Check:* the irrigation schedule.
- **Fruit with a black, sunken base in tomato** — blossom end rot, a calcium
  and water problem, **not** a disease.
  *Check:* whether irrigation is uneven; spraying a fungicide will not fix it.
- **Poor fruit set and heavy flower drop** — heat, water stress, or boron
  shortage. *Check:* the temperatures during flowering and the irrigation of
  that period.
- **Grain partly filled, discoloured, or with a bad smell** — fungal ear or
  panicle disease, or a pest attack at flowering.

### Whole plants and establishment

- **Seedlings collapsing at the soil line and toppling** — damping off, a
  soil-borne problem made worse by over-watering and dense sowing.
- **Seedlings pale and stunted with no root nodules** (in pulses, soybean,
  groundnut) — poor rhizobium nodulation.
  *Check:* dig a plant. Active nodules are pink or reddish inside; white or
  green nodules are not fixing nitrogen.
- **Patchy, stunted plants across the field with poor vigour** — compaction,
  salinity, nematodes, or uneven fertiliser application.

## Crop-wise watch list

- **Rice (paddy):** stem borer, brown planthopper (hoppers at the water line),
  leaf folder, blast, bacterial leaf blight (yellowing and drying from the leaf
  tip and margin). Standing water and dense planting worsen most of these.
- **Wheat:** rusts (wind-borne, act early), aphids, termites, Karnal bunt.
- **Maize:** fall armyworm (whorl damage on young plants), stem borer; check
  the whorl early in the morning.
- **Cotton:** sucking pests early (jassids, thrips, whitefly), pink bollworm
  later in green bolls; do not extend the crop season.
- **Soybean and pulses:** girdle beetle, pod borer, whitefly, yellow mosaic
  virus.
- **Groundnut:** leaf miner, white grub, tikka leaf spot.
- **Sugarcane:** early shoot borer, top borer, red rot, woolly aphid.
- **Tomato:** early and late blight, leaf curl virus (via whitefly),
  fruit borer, blossom end rot (a disorder).
- **Onion:** thrips (silvery streaks and curled leaves), purple blotch,
  stemphylium blight in humid weather, and storage rot in poorly cured bulbs.
- **Chilli:** thrips (upward leaf curl), mites, anthracnose on fruit, damping
  off in the nursery.
- **Brinjal:** shoot and fruit borer, wilt, little leaf virus.
- **Okra:** yellow vein mosaic virus (via whitefly), fruit borer.
- **Banana:** panama wilt, bunchy top (spread by aphids), sigatoka leaf spot.
- **Grape:** downy mildew and powdery mildew in humid conditions.
- **Pomegranate:** bacterial blight (oily, water-soaked spots on fruit).
- **Citrus:** citrus greening (patchy yellowing, lopsided fruit with bitter
  taste), canker, and gummosis.
- **Mango:** hopper, anthracnose in humid weather, powdery mildew at flowering.

## What to do first, whatever the problem

1. **Identify** — confirm the cause before choosing any control.
2. **Remove** — destroy severely infected plants and crop residue away from
   the field. Do not leave them on the bund or in the irrigation channel.
3. **Correct water and nutrition** — many "disease" problems get much worse
   under water stress, waterlogging, or unbalanced fertiliser.
4. **Improve airflow and drainage** — reduce plant density where possible, and
   remove weeds that host pests.
5. **Monitor a fixed set of plants** week by week so you can judge whether your
   action is working instead of spraying blind.
6. **For virus problems:** control the vector and remove infected plants; the
   infected plants themselves cannot be cured.

## When to get local help

Take help from your KVK or block agriculture officer if: the problem is
spreading fast, the cause is still unclear after the checks above, plants are
dying in patches, or you are about to use a pesticide for the first time on
that crop. Carry a few affected plants, including some roots and soil, sealed
in a bag — a diagnosis from a real sample is worth more than any description.
