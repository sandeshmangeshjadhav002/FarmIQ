# Market Prices and Selling the Produce

FarmIQ Starter Reference — how agricultural prices are formed, where to get
today's real prices, and how to get a better price for the same crop.

**FarmIQ cannot give you a live price.** Prices change every day and differ by
market, so anything quoted from memory would be out of date and misleading. The
sections below tell you where the real, current price for your crop and your
district is published, and what the numbers actually mean.

## Where to get the current price

- **AGMARKNET** (agmarknet.gov.in) — daily mandi-wise arrivals and prices
  published by the Directorate of Marketing and Inspection, Government of
  India. Search by crop, state, and district to see yesterday's prices in your
  nearby mandis. This is the standard public reference.
- **eNAM** (enam.gov.in) — the National Agriculture Market; a trading platform
  linking mandis, where you can see bids and trade online after registering.
- **Your local APMC / market committee** — the mandi notice board and the
  auction sheet of the day are the most accurate local record.
- **State agriculture department and market board websites and apps** — most
  states publish daily rates and market advisories.
- **Kisan Call Centre — 1800-180-1551** (toll-free) — for local price and
  market queries in your language.
- **mKisan SMS and state farmer portals** — register to get rates and weather
  advisories by SMS.
- **Your FPO or cooperative** — if you are a member, they often track rates for
  members and can aggregate produce.

## How a mandi price is actually formed

- Crops are normally sold by **open auction** in the mandi. Buyers bid; the
  highest bid wins. The price on a given day depends on how much has arrived
  that day and how many buyers are actively bidding.
- Published rates usually give three figures: **minimum, maximum, and modal
  price**. The **modal price** is the rate at which most of the produce was
  actually sold that day — it is the most useful figure for planning, and the
  minimum and maximum are only the extremes of individual lots.
- **Arrival volume drives price.** Price is usually weakest at the peak of the
  harvest, when everyone brings produce on the same days, and strongest later
  when arrivals thin out. This is the single biggest factor within your
  control.
- **Quality decides your rate within the same market.** Moisture, foreign
  matter, broken or immature grain, size, colour, and variety all move the
  price. Two farmers selling the same crop on the same day can get noticeably
  different rates because of grading.

## Read the deductions, not just the rate

The rate quoted at auction is not what reaches your hand. Ask for, and check,
the full settlement:

- commission or market fee, and any state cess,
- labour and hamali charges, weighing and bagging charges,
- transport from the field or the village to the mandi,
- unloading and loading charges,
- any deduction for moisture, dirt, or damaged produce.

Subtract everything and compare in **net rupees per quintal** between markets
and buyers. A higher quoted rate in a distant mandi can easily become a lower
net rate after transport — always compare like with like.

## MSP and government procurement

- The **Minimum Support Price (MSP)** is announced by the Government of India
  before the sowing season, for the crops it covers. It is a floor price for
  government procurement, **not** a guaranteed price in the open market.
- MSP is relevant to you only if procurement is actually operating for your
  crop in your area, through agencies such as FCI, NAFED, or the Cotton
  Corporation, and if you meet the registration and quality conditions.
- Check with your local agriculture office whether procurement centres are
  open for your crop, what the registration process is, and what quality
  standards and documents are required. Do not assume MSP applies to your crop
  in your district.

## How to get a better price

1. **Grade and clean.** Remove dirt, stones, chaff, damaged and immature grain
   before selling. It costs little and often lifts the rate by more than the
   weight removed.
2. **Dry to safe moisture.** Wet produce is sold at a discount or rejected.
   See the storage reference for target moisture.
3. **Avoid the peak arrival rush.** If the crop stores safely (cereals, pulses,
   oilseeds, onion), selling a few weeks after the harvest peak often pays
   better than selling the day you harvest.
4. **Sell in graded lots, not mixed.** Mixed lots get the lowest common price.
5. **Compare at least three options on the same day:** the nearest mandi, a
   second mandi within reach, and a local trader or processor who buys directly.
   Calculate net rupees per quintal for each, including transport.
6. **Aggregate through your FPO or cooperative.** Bulked, graded volume
   attracts better buyers, and you share transport cost instead of paying it
   alone.
7. **Register on eNAM** so you can bid to buyers in other markets without
   physically carrying the produce there first.
8. **Use warehouse receipts where available.** Storing in a registered (WDRA)
   warehouse gives you a receipt, and banks accept it against a pledge loan.
   That lets you wait for a better price without a cash emergency forcing a
   distress sale.
9. **Keep records.** Record your cost of production per acre — seed, fertiliser,
   plant protection, labour, irrigation, and transport. Without it you cannot
   tell whether a sale price is a profit or a loss.

## What a price question looks like when answered properly

If you ask "what is the price of soybean today", the honest answer is:
*FarmIQ does not hold live prices — check AGMARKNET or your mandi's auction
sheet for today's modal price in your district, and compare net rates after
deductions.* Then ask what the rate means for your decision, and the
comparison above applies: modal price, moisture and grading, transport cost,
and whether waiting is an option for your crop.

## Selling checklist

- Known your cost per quintal, so you know your break-even rate.
- Produce cleaned, graded, and dried to safe moisture.
- Today's modal rate noted from AGMARKNET or the mandi notice board.
- Net rate compared across at least two markets and one direct buyer.
- All deductions written down, not just the headline rate.
- Payment terms confirmed: cash, account transfer, and how many days.
- Receipt, weighment slip, and the buyer's name and licence number retained.
- For stored produce: proper storage, and a plan for what you will sell if the
  price does not improve.
